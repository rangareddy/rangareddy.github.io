TL;DR

  • Start from the consumer and the decision the data supports. Tools come last. An answer that opens with “Kafka, Spark, Snowflake” has skipped the part being graded.
  • State freshness, completeness and correctness as separate targets. “Real time” is not a requirement until it has a number and a consumer.
  • Estimate before you draw: events per second at peak, bytes per day, log disk with replication, lake storage after compression, and the partition count those numbers imply.
  • Exactly-once is a property of a boundary, not of a pipeline. Name the boundary, then make the sink idempotent so that replay is safe.
  • Every design needs a replay story: where the raw data lives, how far back it goes, and how a backfill runs without starving the live path.
  • Monitor the data, not just the jobs. A job that succeeds with zero rows is the most expensive failure in data engineering because nobody gets paged.
  • Close every choice with its cost: “X buys A at the cost of B, and we rejected Y because C”.

How to use this guide

A pipeline that double-counts revenue after a node restart is a system design failure, not a Spark bug. The data engineering design round tests whether you can see that failure coming: whether you ask what one row means, where the data can be replayed from, and which write is allowed to happen twice. This guide gives you a repeatable way to answer that round and the mechanics you need to defend each answer.

It follows the order you would use in the room:

  1. A framework for any design prompt, with a capacity estimate you can do in two minutes.
  2. The building blocks in the order data flows through them: ingestion, the log, processing, delivery guarantees, storage, serving, orchestration and quality.
  3. A runnable demo of the property interviewers probe hardest: a Kafka to Iceberg stream that is replayed after a simulated crash, with one sink that duplicates and one that does not.
  4. Ten worked designs of the prompts that come up most often, from clickstream analytics to GDPR erasure.
  5. Production failures and tuning knobs, so the trade-offs you name are ones you can back with a config key or an error message.

Interview questions with model answers close the framework, building-block, demo, design and tuning sections. Each answer is written the way an interviewer wants to hear it: the decision, the mechanism, and what it costs.

If you have not yet read them, the companion guides cover the ground this one assumes: the data modeling guide for grain, SCD history, CDC patterns and Lambda versus Kappa in depth, and the Structured Streaming internals post for the micro-batch engine the demo runs on.

The core problem and architecture: a framework for any design prompt

Why the data design round is different

A backend design round asks whether a service stays up and answers fast. A data design round asks whether the numbers are still right after the system has failed, been replayed, and been changed by three teams over a year. The state that matters is not a request in flight; it is every row ever written.

Concern Backend system design Data system design
Unit of work A request A record, a batch, or a table commit
Main failure Latency and availability Wrong, missing or duplicated data, often silent
Time Request time Event time, processing time, and the gap between them
Recovery Retry the request Replay a range of history without double-counting
Change Deploy a new version Evolve a schema that years of stored data already use
Success metric p99 latency, error rate Freshness, completeness, correctness, cost per TB

That difference is why the framework below spends two of its seven steps on contracts and recovery, which a backend round barely touches.

The timings are a budget, not a script. What matters is the order: an architecture drawn before step 3 has no grain to be correct about, and a diagram defended before step 5 has never been replayed.

Step 1, the consumer and the decision. Ask who reads the output and what they do with it. “The fraud team blocks a card within 200 ms” and “finance closes the month on day 3” lead to different systems from the same events. Write the consumer and the decision at the top of the whiteboard.

Step 2, numbers and non-functional targets. Turn adjectives into numbers: volume, peak rate, record size, key cardinality, retention, growth. Then set the targets in the next subsection.

Step 3, the data contract. Define the key, the grain (what one row means), the event timestamp, the schema format and its compatibility rule, and who owns it. Most double-counting starts with a grain nobody wrote down.

Step 4, the end-to-end flow. Producers, ingestion, durable log, processing, storage layers, serving, and the control plane around them. Draw it left to right and walk one record through it.

Step 5, correctness and recovery. Delivery semantics at each boundary, deduplication, late data, dead-letter handling, replay and backfill. This is where most candidates lose the round.

Step 6, operations, security and governance. Freshness and volume alerts, lineage, access control, PII masking, retention and deletion, ownership.

Step 7, trade-offs and stress tests. Attack your own design: peak traffic at 10x, a hot key, a source outage of six hours, a breaking schema change, a backfill of a year. Say what bends and what breaks.

Step 2 in practice: freshness, completeness and correctness are three targets

“Real time” fuses three properties that a design trades against each other:

Target Definition Example SLO
Freshness Age of the newest data a consumer can read Dashboard data at most 2 minutes old at p95
Completeness Share of events for a period that have arrived 99.9% of an hour’s events present 15 minutes after the hour
Correctness Each event counted once, at the right grain and time Daily revenue matches the payment ledger to the cent
Latency Time from event to decision Fraud score returned in under 100 ms
Durability Data that may be lost Zero acknowledged events lost
Replayability How far back the raw input can be reprocessed 7 days from the log, 3 years from the lake

A dashboard can be fresh and incomplete at the same time: at 10:02 the 10:00-10:01 bucket is visible but late mobile events have not arrived yet. Stating both targets forces the late-data question that step 5 answers.

Step 2 in practice: capacity estimation

Estimation earns its time when it changes a decision. Do it in this order: average rate, peak rate, bytes per day, log disk, lake storage, and the partition count. Every input is an assumption to say out loud.

"""Back-of-envelope sizing for an event pipeline, as done in an interview.

Every input is an assumption to state out loud. The per-partition budget and
the compression ratio in particular must be measured on your own data.
"""
import math

events_per_day = 2_000_000_000      # stated by the interviewer
avg_event_bytes = 1_000             # JSON, measured from a sample
peak_to_avg = 3                     # evening peak vs daily average
kafka_retention_days = 7
kafka_replication = 3
partition_budget_mb_s = 5           # per-partition write budget, measured
parquet_ratio = 5                   # JSON -> zstd Parquet, measured
lake_retention_days = 365

SECONDS_PER_DAY = 86_400
MB, GB, TB = 10**6, 10**9, 10**12

avg_eps = events_per_day / SECONDS_PER_DAY
avg_mb_s = avg_eps * avg_event_bytes / MB
peak_mb_s = avg_mb_s * peak_to_avg
raw_per_day = events_per_day * avg_event_bytes
kafka_disk = raw_per_day * kafka_retention_days * kafka_replication
lake_per_day = raw_per_day / parquet_ratio
partitions = math.ceil(peak_mb_s / partition_budget_mb_s)

print(f"average rate     : {avg_eps:,.0f} events/s, {avg_mb_s:.1f} MB/s")
print(f"peak rate        : {avg_eps * peak_to_avg:,.0f} events/s, "
      f"{peak_mb_s:.1f} MB/s")
print(f"raw per day      : {raw_per_day / TB:.1f} TB")
print(f"kafka disk       : {kafka_disk / TB:.0f} TB "
      f"({kafka_retention_days} days x RF {kafka_replication})")
print(f"min partitions   : {partitions} at {partition_budget_mb_s} MB/s each")
print(f"lake per day     : {lake_per_day / GB:,.0f} GB Parquet")
print(f"lake per year    : {lake_per_day * lake_retention_days / TB:,.0f} TB")

Run with python3 capacity_estimate.py:

Read the numbers back as decisions. 69 MB/s at peak is a modest Kafka load, so the cluster is sized by retention disk, 42 TB, not by throughput. Fourteen partitions is the floor from throughput; the real count is set by consumer parallelism and headroom, so 24 or 48 is a defensible answer if you say why. 400 GB of Parquet a day means daily partitions hold hundreds of 512 MB files, which is healthy; hourly partitions would hold about 17 GB each, also fine. 146 TB a year puts a lifecycle policy on the table.

The two inputs marked “measured” are the ones an interviewer may challenge. Say that the per-partition budget comes from a load test on your brokers and message sizes, and the compression ratio from writing a day of real data. A number you can explain beats a number that sounds precise.

Three fields carry most of the correctness: a dedup key (event_id), an entity key that becomes the Kafka message key and the upsert key (order_id), and an event time that orders versions of the same entity (updated_ts). Without the last one, “latest wins” means “last to arrive wins”, and a retried old event silently overwrites a newer one. The demo in this guide feeds exactly that stale event into the pipeline.

Walk one record through it. An order row changes in PostgreSQL; the change lands in the write-ahead log; Debezium reads the log and publishes a keyed event to Kafka, which replicates it to three brokers before acknowledging. A stream job reads it, validates it against the registered schema, and appends it to bronze. A second step deduplicates and upserts it into silver at the order grain. Batch jobs build gold aggregates from silver, and a serving store answers dashboards and APIs. Files and SaaS APIs skip the log and land in an object-store landing zone, from which the batch path picks them up.

The bottom band is not decoration. A design without a schema registry, a catalog, quality checks and freshness alerts works on the day it ships and degrades silently afterwards.

Step 5 in practice: correctness and recovery

Answer five questions for every boundary in the diagram:

  1. What are the delivery semantics here? At-most-once, at-least-once, or effectively-once, and what makes it so.
  2. Where is the dedup key, and who applies it? Usually the sink, through a MERGE on the entity key or a unique constraint.
  3. What happens to late data? Updated in place, written to a correction partition, or dropped behind a watermark and counted.
  4. Where do bad records go? A dead-letter topic or table with the error, the raw payload and the source offset, plus an alert on its rate.
  5. How do we replay? From which store, how far back, at what rate, and what stops the replay from overwriting newer data.

Step 7 in practice: trade-off sentences

Interviewers grade the trade-off, not the tool. Use one sentence shape:

“Micro-batches every 30 seconds buy us idempotent, transactional table commits at the cost of about a minute of freshness. We rejected per-record streaming into the warehouse because each record would be a commit.”

Then stress-test: “At 10x the peak, the Kafka cluster holds because it is sized by disk; the stream job falls behind, lag grows, and maxOffsetsPerTrigger keeps each batch bounded while autoscaling catches up.”

Framework interview questions

1. You are asked to "design a real-time analytics pipeline". What do you do in the first five minutes?

I do not draw anything yet. I ask who consumes the output and what decision it drives, the event volume at peak, how fresh the data must be and how complete, whether source records change after they are emitted, and how far back we must be able to reprocess. Then I restate the binding constraint, for example “dashboards two minutes fresh, finance numbers final after one hour”, and get agreement on it. Those answers decide batch versus streaming and how much exactly-once machinery is justified; the tool names come after.

2. How do you choose between batch and streaming?

From the freshness target and the decision it serves, not from preference. If the consumer acts on data within seconds or minutes (fraud, alerting, operational dashboards), stream. If the consumer reads daily or hourly (finance, most BI), batch is cheaper to build, test, backfill and operate. A common middle ground is micro-batch on a schedule, such as Spark Structured Streaming with trigger(availableNow=True) every 15 minutes, which keeps one code path and gets incremental processing without an always-on cluster. I also say what I give up: streaming costs state management, late-data handling and on-call load; batch costs freshness.

3. Walk me through a capacity estimate for 2 billion events a day.

Two billion over 86,400 seconds is about 23,000 events per second on average. At 1 KB an event that is 23 MB/s, and with a 3x evening peak about 70 MB/s. Raw volume is 2 TB a day; seven days in Kafka at replication 3 is 42 TB of broker disk, so retention, not throughput, sizes the cluster. If Parquet compresses the JSON 5x, the lake grows 400 GB a day, roughly 146 TB a year, which justifies lifecycle tiering. Fourteen partitions is the floor at a 5 MB/s per-partition budget, and I would pick 24 or 48 for consumer parallelism and growth. I would flag the compression ratio and partition budget as numbers to measure.

4. What is a data contract, and what goes in one?

A data contract is the producer’s promise about a dataset that consumers can build on. It names the owner, the schema and its compatibility rule (for example backward compatible in the schema registry), the grain, the keys (a unique event id for dedup and an entity key for ordering and upserts), the event-time field and its time zone, allowed values, nullability, and the freshness and volume a consumer can expect. It turns a breaking change into a failed CI check or a rejected registration instead of a broken dashboard.

5. Freshness and completeness: why state both?

Because a pipeline can meet one and fail the other. A dashboard can show data from 30 seconds ago (fresh) while 2% of the last hour’s mobile events are still buffered on phones (incomplete). If I only promise freshness, consumers treat the latest hour as final and make decisions on partial numbers. Stating “fresh within 2 minutes, 99.9% complete 15 minutes after the hour, final at T+1 day” tells consumers when a number can still move, and it forces the design to decide how late data updates published results.

The log: Kafka as the system’s buffer and replay store

A durable log decouples producers from consumers in time. Producers write at their rate, consumers read at theirs, and the log holds the difference. Most data platform designs put one in the middle for three reasons: it absorbs bursts, it lets several consumers read the same events independently, and it is the first replay store.

 topic: orders  (3 partitions, replication factor 3)

 producer: key = order_id ──► partition = hash(key) % 3
                               (Kafka's default partitioner uses murmur2)
 partition 0  [o1 o4 o7 ...]  leader broker 1, followers 2, 3
 partition 1  [o2 o5 o8 ...]  leader broker 2, followers 3, 1
 partition 2  [o3 o6 o9 ...]  leader broker 3, followers 1, 2
               offset 0 1 2 ... append-only, ordered within a partition only

 consumer group "lake-writer"        consumer group "fraud"
   consumer A ◄─ partition 0, 1        consumer X ◄─ partition 0
   consumer B ◄─ partition 2           consumer Y ◄─ partition 1
                                       consumer Z ◄─ partition 2
 each group tracks its own committed offsets in __consumer_offsets

Read it from the top. The producer hashes the message key to pick a partition, so every event for o1 lands in partition 0 and stays in order relative to other o1 events. Ordering holds within a partition only; there is no global order across the topic. Each partition has a leader that takes writes and followers that copy it. A consumer group splits the partitions among its members, so a group can have at most as many active consumers as there are partitions, and the extra members sit idle. Each group commits its own offsets, which is why the fraud team and the lake writer can read the same topic at different speeds.

The settings below decide whether an acknowledged event can be lost:

Setting Scope Default Design choice
replication.factor Topic 1 (default.replication.factor) 3 in production
min.insync.replicas Topic / broker 1 2 with RF 3: a write needs two copies
acks Producer all since Kafka 3.0 all; 1 loses data if the leader dies before followers copy
enable.idempotence Producer true since Kafka 3.0 Keep it; retries no longer create duplicates in the log
retention.ms Topic 604800000 (7 days) Sized from the replay window and disk
isolation.level Consumer read_uncommitted read_committed when producers use transactions

acks=all with min.insync.replicas=2 and RF 3 means an acknowledged write exists on at least two brokers, and the cluster tolerates one broker loss without refusing writes. The cost is write latency: the leader waits for a follower. The idempotent producer attaches a producer id and a sequence number per partition, so a retried batch that the broker already has is discarded rather than appended twice. It removes duplicates caused by producer retries; it does nothing for an application that sends the same business event twice, which is why the contract still carries an event_id.

Choosing the key. The key decides ordering and load. Key by the entity whose events must stay ordered (order_id, account_id, device_id). If one key dominates, such as one huge merchant, its partition becomes hot. The fixes are a composite key (merchant_id plus a bucket) when per-merchant order does not matter, or a dedicated topic for the outlier. Increasing the partition count later changes hash(key) % n, so keys move partitions and per-key ordering breaks across the change. Over-provision partitions up front instead.

Processing: batch, micro-batch and continuous

Model Unit Latency State Failure recovery Engines
Batch A bounded input, often a date partition Minutes to hours Recomputed each run Re-run the partition Spark, dbt, Trino
Micro-batch A range of offsets per trigger Seconds to minutes Checkpointed state store Replay the last uncommitted batch Spark Structured Streaming

The micro-batch engine is worth knowing in detail, because its recovery rule is what the demo exercises:

 Spark Structured Streaming, one micro-batch (batch N)

  1. plan    ── read latest Kafka offsets ──► write offsets/N to checkpoint
  2. execute ── read offsets(N-1)..offsets(N) ──► transform ──► sink
  3. commit  ── write commits/N to checkpoint
                ▲
                └─ crash here: offsets/N exists, commits/N does not.
                   On restart, Spark re-runs batch N with the SAME offsets.

The offsets log is written before the batch runs (a write-ahead log) and the commit log after the sink returns. If the process dies between steps 2 and 3, the restart finds an offset entry without a commit entry and re-executes batch N over exactly the same offset range. The engine guarantees the replay is the same input. Whether the output is duplicated depends entirely on the sink. A sink that appends writes the batch twice; a sink that upserts on a key writes the same final state twice, which is the same as writing it once.

Event time and watermarks. For aggregations by event time, the engine has to decide when a window is complete. A watermark is that decision: “no event older than the maximum event time seen minus 10 minutes is expected”. withWatermark("event_ts", "10 minutes") lets Spark finalise windows and drop state behind the watermark. Events older than the watermark are dropped by stateful operators. That is a correctness trade-off you should state: a larger delay keeps more state and publishes later; a smaller one drops more late data. Count the late events you drop, or route them to a correction path, rather than losing them silently.

State. Windowed aggregations, stream-stream joins and streaming deduplication keep state per key. Spark’s default state store provider is HDFSBackedStateStoreProvider, which holds state in executor JVM memory and checkpoints it to files. For large state, switch spark.sql.streaming.stateStore.providerClass to org.apache.spark.sql.execution.streaming.state.RocksDBStateStoreProvider, which keeps it in native RocksDB instances outside the JVM heap. Spark 3.5 also added dropDuplicatesWithinWatermark, which deduplicates on a key while bounding state with the watermark.

Delivery semantics: where exactly-once actually lives

Guarantee Meaning How you get it Cost
At-most-once Each event is processed zero or one times Commit offsets before processing Data loss on crash
At-least-once Each event is processed one or more times Commit offsets after the sink succeeds Duplicates on replay
Effectively-once Each event affects the output once At-least-once delivery plus an idempotent or transactional sink Sink design and keys

“Exactly-once” is always scoped to a boundary. The mechanisms differ by boundary:

Boundary Mechanism
Producer to Kafka Idempotent producer (sequence numbers per partition)
Kafka to Kafka Kafka transactions: consume, produce and commit offsets atomically; readers use read_committed
Kafka to a table format Replayable source, deterministic batch, idempotent MERGE or a sink that records the batch id it committed
Kafka to a database Write the rows and the consumed offset in one database transaction
Anything to an external API Idempotency key on the request; the API must deduplicate
Flink to Kafka or files Two-phase commit tied to checkpoints (KafkaSink with DeliveryGuarantee.EXACTLY_ONCE)

The sentence interviewers want: “Delivery is at-least-once everywhere; I make the effect exactly-once at the sink by upserting on a deterministic key, so a replay rewrites the same rows.”

Storage: lake, warehouse and lakehouse tables

A plain data lake is a directory of Parquet files: cheap, open, and with no transactions. Two writers can interleave, a reader can see half a job’s output, and an update means rewriting a partition by hand. A warehouse gives transactions and fast SQL but stores data in its own format, so every other engine pays to copy it out. An open table format (Iceberg, Hudi, Delta Lake) adds a transaction log over Parquet files in object storage, which is what a lakehouse is.

 Iceberg table commit: an atomic swap of one pointer

 catalog: shop.orders ──► metadata/v12.metadata.json   (current)
                               │ snapshots: [..., s11, s12]
                               ▼
                         snap-s12 manifest list (.avro)
                          ├─► manifest A (.avro) ──► day=2026-10-28/00001.parquet
                          │                      ──► day=2026-10-28/00002.parquet
                          └─► manifest B (.avro) ──► day=2026-10-27/00417.parquet

 writer: write new data files ──► write manifests ──► write v13.metadata.json
         ──► ask catalog: "swap v12 → v13 if current is still v12"
             success: readers see s13 atomically
             conflict: re-read, re-validate, retry (commit.retry.num-retries)

Writers never modify files in place. A commit writes new files and then compare-and-swaps the catalog pointer to a new metadata file. Readers resolve the pointer once and read a consistent snapshot, so they never see a half write. Concurrent writers use optimistic concurrency: the loser of the swap re-reads, checks that its change does not conflict, and retries up to commit.retry.num-retries times (default 4). Because each commit is a metadata file and a manifest, a design that commits every second creates thousands of snapshots and tiny files an hour. That is why streaming into a table format uses micro-batches of tens of seconds or more, plus scheduled compaction and snapshot expiry.

Orchestration and backfills

An orchestrator (Airflow, Dagster, or a managed equivalent) runs batch steps in dependency order, retries them and records what ran. Two properties make a DAG safe to retry and to backfill:

Tasks are idempotent per partition. Each run writes one logical partition, for example order_date = 2026-10-27, with an overwrite or a MERGE, never a blind append. Re-running the same date gives the same result.

Inputs are pinned. A run for 2026-10-27 reads the inputs for that date, not “everything new since the last run”. Then a backfill of last March is the same code with a different date, and it can run in parallel with a separate compute pool so it does not starve the live schedule.

For streaming jobs, the backfill is a replay: a second instance of the job with its own checkpoint reads a historical range (from the lake if the log has expired) and writes through the same idempotent sink.

Building-block interview questions

6. Why put Kafka between the database and the lake instead of writing straight to the lake?

Three reasons. It decouples rates: a slow lake commit or a compaction does not push back on the source. It allows several independent consumers, such as the lake writer, a search indexer and a fraud service, to read the same changes with their own offsets. And it is a replay buffer: if a bug corrupts silver, I can rewind a consumer group and reprocess the retained days. The cost is another system to operate and size, mostly by retention disk. For a single consumer with hourly freshness I might skip it and land CDC files directly.

7. How does Kafka guarantee ordering, and when does it not?

Ordering is per partition. The producer picks the partition from the message key, so all events with one key go to one partition in send order, and a consumer reads a partition in offset order. There is no order across partitions. Ordering breaks if producers use no key, if the partition count is increased (keys rehash to different partitions), or if a non-idempotent producer with several in-flight requests retries a failed batch after a later one succeeded. The idempotent producer, the default since Kafka 3.0, preserves order for up to five in-flight requests.

8. Explain at-least-once versus exactly-once in a Spark streaming job writing to a table.

Spark’s source and checkpoint give replayable, deterministic input: the offsets for batch N are written before it runs, and on restart an uncommitted batch N is re-run over the same offsets. So delivery to the sink is at-least-once. The effect is exactly-once only if the sink tolerates the replay. An Iceberg MERGE on the entity key does: replaying the batch writes the same final rows. A plain append does not: it adds the batch twice. I would show this with a deleted commit file, which is what the demo below does.

9. What is a watermark, and what does it cost?

A watermark is the engine’s rule for when event-time results are final: the maximum event time seen minus an allowed delay. It lets the engine emit windows and discard state. The cost is late data: events older than the watermark are dropped by stateful operators. A long delay keeps more state and publishes later; a short one drops more. I size it from the observed lateness distribution of the slowest source, count what is dropped, and route very late events to a batch correction job if the numbers must be exact.

10. How does an open table format make a commit atomic on object storage?

Files are immutable. A writer writes its new data files and manifests, then a new metadata file, and finally asks the catalog to swap the table’s pointer from the old metadata file to the new one, only if the pointer has not moved. That compare-and-swap is the commit. Readers resolve the pointer once and see a full snapshot. If another writer moved the pointer first, the loser re-reads, checks for conflicts with what changed, and retries. Object storage only has to store files; the catalog provides the atomic step.

11. How do you make a batch pipeline safe to re-run and to backfill?

Each task writes one logical partition with overwrite or MERGE, never append, so a re-run replaces rather than adds. Each run reads inputs pinned to its logical date, not “since last run”, so a backfill is the same code with a different date. Backfills run on separate compute, throttled, so the live schedule keeps its SLO. And I check the result with the same quality gates, including a reconciliation against the source for the backfilled range.

12. What would you monitor on a new pipeline on day one?

Freshness (max event time against now), volume against the same period last week, schema drift against the contract, a handful of validity checks on keys and amounts, and consumer lag for streaming jobs. Job success alone is not enough: the worst incidents are jobs that succeed with zero or half the rows. Each alert has an owner and lineage, so it says which consumers are affected. P1 alerts page someone; data-quality warnings open tickets.

"""Publish order events to Kafka for the system design demo.

Run 1 sends 1,000 new orders plus 50 exact duplicates, the way a producer
retry after a lost ack would. Run 2 sends 500 new orders, 30 status updates
for run-1 orders, and 10 stale updates that arrive out of order.
"""
import sys
from datetime import datetime, timedelta

from pyspark.sql import SparkSession

TOPIC = "sd_blog_orders"
BOOTSTRAP = "kafka:29092"
BASE = datetime(2026, 10, 28, 10, 0, 0)


def order(i, status, updated):
    return {
        "event_id": f"o{i:05d}-{status}",
        "order_id": f"o{i:05d}",
        "customer_id": f"c{i % 97:03d}",
        "amount": round(10 + (i * 7.31) % 490, 2),
        "status": status,
        "order_ts": (BASE + timedelta(seconds=i * 2)).isoformat(),
        "updated_ts": updated.isoformat(),
    }


def run_1():
    rows = [order(i, "PLACED", BASE + timedelta(seconds=i * 2))
            for i in range(1, 1001)]
    return rows + rows[:50]                       # retried sends


def run_2():
    rows = [order(i, "PLACED", BASE + timedelta(seconds=i * 2))
            for i in range(1001, 1501)]
    shipped_at = BASE + timedelta(hours=2)
    rows += [order(i, "SHIPPED", shipped_at) for i in range(1, 31)]
    # Older than the PLACED event already stored: must not win.
    rows += [order(i, "PENDING", BASE - timedelta(hours=1))
             for i in range(31, 41)]
    return rows


if __name__ == "__main__":
    spark = SparkSession.builder.appName("sd-produce-orders").getOrCreate()
    rows = run_1() if sys.argv[1] == "1" else run_2()
    df = spark.createDataFrame(rows)
    (df.selectExpr("order_id AS key", "to_json(struct(*)) AS value")
       .write.format("kafka")
       .option("kafka.bootstrap.servers", BOOTSTRAP)
       .option("topic", TOPIC)
       .save())
    print(f"published {len(rows)} events to {TOPIC}")
    spark.stop()

Step 3: the streaming job

Inside foreachBatch, each micro-batch goes to both sinks. Before the MERGE, a window keeps one row per order_id within the batch, the newest by updated_ts. That step is required, not a nicety: a MERGE whose source has two rows for one target row fails, as the last step shows. The MERGE itself only updates when the incoming version is newer, which is what rejects the stale events.

"""Kafka -> Spark Structured Streaming -> Iceberg, with an idempotent sink.

Each micro-batch is written twice: a naive append to orders_append, and a
deduplicating MERGE into orders keyed on order_id. Replaying a batch shows
which of the two sinks survives a crash between write and checkpoint commit.
"""
from pyspark.sql import SparkSession, Window
from pyspark.sql import functions as F
from pyspark.sql.types import (DoubleType, StringType, StructField,
                               StructType, TimestampType)

WAREHOUSE = "file:///tmp/sd_blog/warehouse"
CHECKPOINT = "file:///tmp/sd_blog/checkpoints/orders"

spark = (
    SparkSession.builder.appName("sd-orders-stream")
    .config("spark.sql.extensions",
            "org.apache.iceberg.spark.extensions."
            "IcebergSparkSessionExtensions")
    .config("spark.serializer",
            "org.apache.spark.serializer.KryoSerializer")
    .config("spark.sql.catalog.sd", "org.apache.iceberg.spark.SparkCatalog")
    .config("spark.sql.catalog.sd.type", "hadoop")
    .config("spark.sql.catalog.sd.warehouse", WAREHOUSE)
    .config("spark.sql.shuffle.partitions", "4")
    .getOrCreate()
)

spark.sql("CREATE NAMESPACE IF NOT EXISTS sd.shop")
for table in ("orders", "orders_append"):
    spark.sql(f"""
        CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS sd.shop.{table} (
            order_id STRING, customer_id STRING, amount DOUBLE,
            status STRING, order_ts TIMESTAMP, updated_ts TIMESTAMP,
            kafka_partition INT, kafka_offset BIGINT)
        USING iceberg
        PARTITIONED BY (days(order_ts))""")

schema = StructType([
    StructField("event_id", StringType()),
    StructField("order_id", StringType()),
    StructField("customer_id", StringType()),
    StructField("amount", DoubleType()),
    StructField("status", StringType()),
    StructField("order_ts", TimestampType()),
    StructField("updated_ts", TimestampType()),
])

events = (
    spark.readStream.format("kafka")
    .option("kafka.bootstrap.servers", "kafka:29092")
    .option("subscribe", "sd_blog_orders")
    .option("startingOffsets", "earliest")
    .option("maxOffsetsPerTrigger", "400")
    .load()
    .select(F.from_json(F.col("value").cast("string"), schema).alias("e"),
            F.col("partition").alias("kafka_partition"),
            F.col("offset").alias("kafka_offset"))
    .select("e.*", "kafka_partition", "kafka_offset")
    .drop("event_id")
)


def write_batch(batch_df, batch_id):
    batch_df.persist()
    batch_df.writeTo("sd.shop.orders_append").append()

    # Keep the newest version of each order inside the batch, so MERGE
    # never sees two source rows for one target row.
    latest = Window.partitionBy("order_id").orderBy(
        F.col("updated_ts").desc(), F.col("kafka_offset").desc())
    (batch_df.withColumn("rn", F.row_number().over(latest))
        .where("rn = 1").drop("rn")
        .createOrReplaceTempView("batch_orders"))
    batch_df.sparkSession.sql("""
        MERGE INTO sd.shop.orders t
        USING batch_orders s
        ON t.order_id = s.order_id
        WHEN MATCHED AND s.updated_ts > t.updated_ts THEN UPDATE SET *
        WHEN NOT MATCHED THEN INSERT *""")
    print(f"batch {batch_id}: {batch_df.count()} events")
    batch_df.unpersist()


query = (
    events.writeStream
    .foreachBatch(write_batch)
    .option("checkpointLocation", CHECKPOINT)
    .trigger(availableNow=True)
    .start()
)
query.awaitTermination()

for table in ("orders", "orders_append"):
    # foreachBatch writes through a cloned session with its own catalog
    # cache; without REFRESH this session still sees the empty table.
    spark.sql(f"REFRESH TABLE sd.shop.{table}")
    n = spark.table(f"sd.shop.{table}").count()
    print(f"{table}: {n} rows")
spark.sql("""SELECT status, count(*) AS orders FROM sd.shop.orders
             GROUP BY status ORDER BY status""").show()
spark.stop()

The full error is SparkRuntimeException: [MERGE_CARDINALITY_VIOLATION] The ON search condition of the MERGE statement matched a single row from the target table with multiple rows of the source table. In a streaming job this fails the batch, and every restart replays the same batch and fails again, so the query is stuck until the code changes. Deduplicate inside each batch before merging.

What the demo proves, and a pitfall it found

Replay is the engine’s job; idempotency is yours. Spark re-ran batch 2 with identical input, as its checkpoint protocol promises. Only the sink design decided whether that produced duplicates.

The catalog cache pitfall. The first version of orders_stream.py counted the tables at the end without REFRESH TABLE and printed 0 rows for both, although the data was there. Structured Streaming runs each query in a cloned SparkSession, and foreachBatch hands you a DataFrame bound to that clone, whose Iceberg catalog has its own table cache. The driver session still held the table it loaded at creation time, before any batch committed. REFRESH TABLE invalidates the cached entry. A monitoring job that reads row counts from a long-lived session can report stale numbers in the same way.

Demo interview questions

13. Your streaming job writes to a table with foreachBatch. How do you make it exactly-once?

foreachBatch gives at-least-once: an uncommitted batch is re-run with the same offsets and the same batch_id. I make the write idempotent. Either I MERGE on the entity key after deduplicating within the batch, so a replay rewrites the same rows, or I use a sink that records the batch id it committed and skips a repeat (Delta Lake supports this with the txnAppId and txnVersion writer options). A plain append inside foreachBatch duplicates the batch on replay.

14. An old event arrives after a newer one for the same order. What protects the table?

The event’s own time, not the arrival order. The contract carries updated_ts, and the MERGE updates only WHEN MATCHED AND s.updated_ts > t.updated_ts. Within a batch, a window keeps the newest version per key, with the Kafka offset as a tie-breaker. Without the guard, “latest wins” means “last to arrive wins”, and a retried or delayed event rolls the order back.

                                                       5-minute dashboards

Key decisions. The collector batches events and returns quickly; mobile SDKs buffer offline, so expect events hours or days late and partition bronze by event hour while accepting writes to old partitions. Sessionisation is a stateful operation keyed by user with an inactivity gap, typically 30 minutes; run it in the stream with a watermark, or hourly in batch if five-minute freshness only applies to page-level counts. Bot traffic is filtered in silver with a flag, not dropped, so the rule can change later.

Failure modes. A viral page makes one key hot if you key by page; key by session instead. A mobile release that floods duplicate events is absorbed by event_id dedup. Identity resolution (anonymous id to logged-in user) changes history, so keep it as a mapping table joined at read time rather than rewriting events.

Design 2: CDC from an OLTP database to the lakehouse in under five minutes

Prompt. Replicate 300 PostgreSQL tables into the lakehouse with under five minutes of lag, including deletes, for analytics.

Key decisions. Debezium takes a consistent snapshot, then streams from the replication slot, so the lake starts from a known point. Order changes for a row by the source log position (the LSN for PostgreSQL), not by arrival time. Keep bronze append-only with every change event; silver is the MERGE result. Schema changes go through the registry with backward compatibility, and a column drop at the source becomes a nullable column in the lake rather than a failure. Merge-on-read tables suit high-update tables; compact them on a schedule.

Failure modes. If Debezium stops, the replication slot keeps WAL on the primary until the disk fills; cap it with max_slot_wal_keep_size and alert on slot lag. A large table’s initial snapshot can take hours; incremental snapshots let streaming continue during it. Reconcile row counts per table per hour against the source.

Design 3: a real-time operations dashboard with a 10-second SLA

Prompt. Show orders per minute, revenue and error rates per region with data at most 10 seconds old.

Key decisions. Ten seconds rules out lakehouse commits as the serving path, so the stream job aggregates in tumbling or sliding windows and writes the results to a low-latency store: an OLAP engine that ingests from Kafka directly (Pinot, Druid, ClickHouse with a Kafka engine) or a key-value store holding the current window counts with a TTL. The same events still land in the lakehouse for history and for correcting late data. The dashboard reads the fast store; anything older than a day reads gold.

Failure modes. Late events update a window already shown; decide whether the dashboard corrects old buckets or rolls forward, and say which. A burst that causes consumer lag makes the dashboard stale silently; show the data’s age on the dashboard itself.

Design 4: log aggregation for 10,000 servers

Assumptions and estimate. 10,000 servers at 5,000 lines a second each is 50 million lines a second; at 300 bytes a line that is 15 GB/s, about 1.3 PB a day before compression. The estimate is the design: at this volume, sampling and tiering are not optional.

Filtering and sampling happen at the edge, because shipping and then dropping costs network and broker disk. Prompt. Score card transactions at 5,000 a second with features such as “transactions for this card in the last 10 minutes” and “distance from the last transaction”.

Key decisions. The decision path is synchronous and reads precomputed features; nothing heavy runs inside the 100 ms. Features are computed by a stateful stream keyed by card, so all of one card’s events go to one task. The same feature definitions feed the offline store with point-in-time joins, so training sees what the model would have seen at decision time; mismatched logic between the two is training-serving skew.

Failure modes. If the feature store is slow, the scoring service needs a fallback rule set and a timeout, decided with the business: allow, block, or review. Feature freshness lag shows up as missed fraud, so alert on it.

Design 6: IoT telemetry from one million devices

Assumptions and estimate. One million devices reporting every 5 seconds is 200,000 messages a second; at 200 bytes each, 40 MB/s.

Key decisions. Devices speak MQTT to a broker built for many long-lived connections, which bridges to Kafka keyed by device_id. The stream computes per-device rolling statistics and threshold alerts with keyed state (RocksDB for a million keys). Recent raw data goes to a time-series store with downsampling; everything lands in the lakehouse partitioned by date, sorted or clustered by device for per-device queries.

Failure modes. Devices reconnect after an outage and send hours of buffered data at once, so the watermark must tolerate it or the late data must go through a batch path. Device clocks drift; record both device time and ingestion time.

Prompt. Customers upload CSV files of up to 5 GB; parse, validate, store and report errors back to them.

Key decisions. Uploads go straight to object storage through pre-signed URLs, so the API never carries file bytes. Each tenant gets quotas and a separate prefix, and tenant id is a column in every table for isolation and deletion.

Design 8: an e-commerce warehouse for daily reporting

Prompt. 2 million orders a day across web and stores; finance, marketing and operations dashboards refreshed daily, with history kept as it was.

Key decisions. This is a batch design, and saying so early is a strength: 2 million orders a day is about 23 a second. Ingest with CDC or daily extracts into bronze, build silver with dbt or Spark, and model gold as a star schema: fact_order_line at one row per order line, conformed dim_customer and dim_product with SCD Type 2 history, and a date dimension. Partition the facts by order date. Separate compute for loading and for BI queries so a heavy dashboard does not delay the load. The data modeling guide covers this model in full.

Failure modes. Returns and cancellations arrive days later and change past totals; decide whether gold restates history or records adjustments. A fact joined to the current dimension version instead of the version valid at order time rewrites history.

Design 9: a data quality platform for 500 tables

Key decisions. Checks are declared next to the data, as YAML or dbt tests in version control, owned by the dataset owner. The orchestrator runs them as a stage after each load. Gold checks block publishing; bronze checks warn. Results go to a metrics store with history, so the platform can detect drift (row counts, null rates) against the past instead of fixed thresholds alone. Lineage connects a failed check to downstream consumers, and the catalog shows each table’s health.

Failure modes. Alert fatigue: hundreds of low-value checks get ignored. Start with freshness, volume and key uniqueness on the most-used tables, and add checks after incidents.

Design 10: GDPR right to erasure across the lake

Prompt. Delete a user’s personal data within 30 days of a request, across Kafka, the lakehouse, backups and derived tables.

Key decisions. Keep PII in as few tables as possible, keyed by a user id, and refer to users elsewhere by a pseudonymous key. Then erasure is a delete in those tables plus a rewrite of affected files: a table format DELETE writes new files or delete files, and only snapshot expiry and orphan-file cleanup physically remove the old bytes. Old snapshots still contain the data until they expire, so the expiry schedule is part of the compliance window. Kafka retention shorter than the erasure window avoids rewriting topics. An alternative is crypto-shredding: encrypt each user’s PII with a per-user key and delete the key.

Failure modes. Time travel and backups resurrect deleted data; document how long each retains it. Derived aggregates that cannot identify a person can stay; derived tables with user-level rows must be included.

Scenario interview questions

15. Design a pipeline that ingests clickstream data and serves 5-minute dashboards. What are the hardest parts?

Late and duplicate events, sessionisation state, and keeping two serving paths consistent. Mobile clients send events hours late, so bronze is partitioned by event time and accepts writes to old partitions, and gold numbers for recent hours are marked provisional. Duplicates from SDK retries are removed by event_id at the silver MERGE. Sessions need keyed state with an inactivity gap and a watermark. The dashboards read an OLAP store fed by the stream; history reads gold, and a daily reconciliation compares the two.

16. Your CDC connector was down for six hours. What happens, and how do you recover?

On PostgreSQL the replication slot holds WAL since the connector’s last confirmed position, so nothing is lost as long as the primary has disk; that is why slot lag is a P1 alert and max_slot_wal_keep_size is set, accepting that an invalidated slot means a re-snapshot. When the connector restarts it resumes from the slot, Kafka absorbs the burst, and the stream job catches up, bounded by maxOffsetsPerTrigger so batches stay a manageable size. The silver MERGE is ordered by LSN, so replayed changes apply correctly. Then I reconcile counts for the window.

17. How would you handle a request to delete one user's data from the lakehouse?

I would have designed for it: PII lives in a few tables keyed by user id, and everything else uses a pseudonymous key. The deletion job runs a table-format DELETE on those tables, which writes new files or delete files. The old bytes remain in earlier snapshots, so snapshot expiry and orphan-file removal must run within the compliance window, and time travel beyond it is not allowed for those tables. Kafka retention is shorter than the window. For data that cannot be rewritten, per-user encryption keys allow crypto-shredding.

18. When would you not use Kafka?

When there is one consumer, freshness is hourly or daily, and the source can be re-read. A daily extract or CDC files landed in object storage and loaded in batch are simpler and cheaper. Kafka earns its operating cost when several consumers need the same events, when bursts must be absorbed, or when sub- minute freshness and replay from a log are requirements.

Optimisation, tuning knobs and best practices

Partitioning and skew

Partition storage by the column most queries filter on, usually a date or hour of event time, and keep partitions large enough to hold files of hundreds of megabytes. Over-partitioning (by date and user id, say) creates millions of tiny files. Within partitions, sort or cluster by a second frequent filter so file-level min and max statistics can skip files.

For skewed joins, Spark’s adaptive query execution splits oversized shuffle partitions. It is on by default (spark.sql.adaptive.enabled = true, spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin.enabled = true). A partition is treated as skewed when it is larger than spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin.skewedPartitionFactor (5.0) times the median and larger than spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin.skewedPartitionThresholdInBytes (256 MB). When the skew is a known hot key, salting it or handling it in a separate branch is more predictable. The data skew post covers both.

Backpressure and rate limits

When consumers fall behind, the log absorbs it, and consumer lag is the signal. Act on it in order: scale the bottleneck stage, bound the batch size so a backlog does not create one enormous batch (maxOffsetsPerTrigger for Spark’s Kafka source), and shed or defer low-priority work. A backlog larger than retention is data loss, so alert on lag as time (“40 minutes behind”) against the retention window, not as a message count.

Small files and table maintenance

Each streaming commit adds files and a snapshot. Schedule maintenance per table:

Task Iceberg procedure Why
Compact small files CALL sd.system.rewrite_data_files('shop.orders') Fewer, larger files; faster scans
Expire snapshots CALL sd.system.expire_snapshots('shop.orders', TIMESTAMP '2026-10-21 00:00:00') Bounds metadata and frees storage
Remove orphan files CALL sd.system.remove_orphan_files(table => 'shop.orders') Deletes files left by failed writes
Rewrite manifests CALL sd.system.rewrite_manifests('shop.orders') Faster planning on tables with many commits

Iceberg’s default snapshot age for expiry is 5 days (history.expire.max-snapshot-age-ms = 432000000), which also bounds how far back time travel works after expiry runs.

Tuning interview questions

19. Your streaming table has 200,000 small files. How did that happen, and what do you do?

Each micro-batch commit writes at least one file per written partition per task, so a short trigger interval, many shuffle partitions and fine-grained partitioning multiply into many small files. I would lengthen the trigger interval if freshness allows, reduce the number of writing tasks or use a hash distribution so each partition gets fewer files, and schedule rewrite_data_files for compaction plus expire_snapshots and orphan-file cleanup. I would also check whether partitioning is too fine for the volume.

20. Consumer lag keeps growing. Walk me through the diagnosis.

First, is it all partitions or a few? A few means skew: a hot key or an uneven partition assignment. All partitions means throughput: check whether the job is CPU-bound, waiting on the sink (slow commits, a throttled database), or rebalancing because processing exceeds max.poll.interval.ms. Then act: scale the bottleneck, bound batch sizes, fix the hot key, or add partitions with a plan for the key remapping. Throughout, I compare lag in time with the retention window, because a backlog beyond retention loses data.

21. How would you cut the cost of a 50 TB-a-day platform?

Measure first: compute, storage, log retention and data transfer each need a number. Then the usual levers: run streams that only need 15-minute freshness as scheduled micro-batches instead of always-on clusters; use spot or preemptible capacity for batch with retries; store columnar with zstd and lifecycle old partitions to colder tiers; compact and cluster so queries scan less; pre-aggregate the most expensive dashboard queries into gold tables; and keep traffic in one region. Each change gets a before and after number.

Summary and key takeaways

A data system design answer is judged on what happens after the happy path: the restart, the replay, the late event, the schema change, the bill. The framework puts those questions in the order an interviewer expects. Consumer and decision first, then numbers, then the contract, then the diagram, then recovery, operations and trade-offs.

The mental model to carry into the room:

  • The log and the lake are your replay stores. Know how far back each goes, and design every sink so that replaying into it is safe.
  • Delivery is at-least-once; effects are made exactly-once at the sink. The demo showed it: Spark replayed batch 2 with identical input, the append table grew from 1,050 to 1,304 rows, and the MERGE table stayed at 1,000.
  • Event time orders versions; arrival time does not. A version guard in the MERGE rejected all 10 stale events.
  • Commits are the unit of cost in a lakehouse. Batch them, compact them, and expire them.
  • Quality is monitored on data. Freshness, volume, schema, validity and reconciliation, with owners and lineage.

Practise each worked design out loud against a 40-minute timer. For each one, say the binding requirement, the estimate, the replay story and one rejected alternative. If you can do that for all ten, the specific prompt matters much less.
